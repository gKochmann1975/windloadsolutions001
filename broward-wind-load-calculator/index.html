Broward County is a single uniform wind load jurisdiction. From the high-rise oceanfront condominiums at Galt Ocean Mile down to the inland subdivisions of Pembroke Pines and the gated communities of Weston, every parcel inside the county line falls under the same High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) rules and the same 170 mph design wind speed for Risk Category II construction. There is no "inland Broward" exemption — the HVHZ boundary follows the county boundary all the way to the Everglades.

That uniformity is helpful for designers: once you know the building is in Broward, you know the wind speed, the product approval pathway (NOA + TAS testing), and which authority administers the permit (Broward County Building Code Services Division, often delegated to one of the 31 incorporated municipalities). Why Broward is its own page

Broward shares HVHZ status with Miami-Dade, but they are not the same jurisdiction. Different design wind speed (170 vs 175), different administering authority, different amendments, different submittal forms. A calculator output that says "Florida HVHZ" without distinguishing Broward from Miami-Dade can fail a Fort Lauderdale plan review even when the underlying physics is correct. This page (and the calculator behind it) treat Broward as Broward.

Broward Inside the HVHZ: What Actually Changes

Broward and Miami-Dade are the only two counties in Florida designated as the High Velocity Hurricane Zone. The HVHZ provisions are baked into the Florida Building Code itself (Sections 1626 and related chapters) and have been since the post-Hurricane Andrew reforms of the mid-1990s. Inside Broward, four things work differently than in the rest of Florida:

  • Product approval pathway. Every window, sliding glass door, entry door, garage door, roof tile, shutter, and impact-rated assembly installed in Broward must carry an FL# whose underlying approval is HVHZ-compliant — almost always a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA). Broward accepts the Miami-Dade NOA as the canonical approval document.
  • Test protocols. Products must pass TAS 201 (Large Missile Impact — typically a 9 lb 2x4 fired at 50 ft/sec), TAS 202 (Uniform Static Air Pressure to test at +1.5 design pressure), and TAS 203 (Cyclic Wind Pressure — 9,000 cycles at progressive pressure levels). These three tests together define an "HVHZ-rated" product.
  • Plan submittal expectations. Broward plan reviewers expect to see each opening on a wind load report cross-referenced to a specific FL# / NOA whose tested design pressure equals or exceeds the calculator's output for that opening's zone. A wind load report that lists pressures without product approval matching is incomplete.
  • Inspection sequencing. Broward inspectors verify NOA installation instructions on site — anchor type, anchor spacing, substrate, sealant pattern — to the letter of the NOA. Installing a compliant product incorrectly is a fail.

Outside the HVHZ (the other 65 Florida counties), a product can be Florida-approved without going through the TAS 201/202/203 gauntlet. Inside Broward, it cannot. This is why HVHZ-rated impact windows are roughly two to three times the cost of comparable non-HVHZ impact windows: the testing alone costs the manufacturer a substantial amount per product line, and recertification is required when components change.

Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) in Broward

The Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) took effect statewide on December 31, 2024, replacing the 7th Edition (2020). The 8th Edition references ASCE 7-22 as the wind load standard. For Broward specifically, the relevant interpretive guidance is published as Florida Building Information (FI) bulletins; the current Broward-authority document for 8th Edition application is FI-24, which addresses transition rules, HVHZ amendment clarifications, and product approval matching under the new edition.

What changed between 7th Edition / ASCE 7-16 and 8th Edition / ASCE 7-22 that matters for Broward work:

  • Wind speed maps updated. ASCE 7-22 refreshed the underlying hurricane climatology and adjusted some return-period analyses. Broward's 170 mph county floor was unchanged in the adoption process — the floor sits above the baseline either way.
  • Four enclosure classifications instead of three. ASCE 7-22 introduced "Partially Open" as a fourth enclosure category alongside Enclosed, Partially Enclosed, and Open. This matters in Broward for screen enclosures, lanais, and partially screened pool cages, which previously got squeezed into the wrong bucket.
  • Edge strip "a" minimum at 4 feet. Florida (per FBC R301.2(7)) requires a 4-foot minimum edge strip dimension for residential C&C zones, where ASCE 7-22 elsewhere allows 3 feet. This applies in Broward.
  • Reorganized Components and Cladding chapter. ASCE 7-22 Chapter 30 restructured C&C pressure procedures across six roof geometries. The calculator handles all six and applies the correct procedure based on roof shape, slope, and mean roof height.

WindLoadCalc is FBC 8th Edition / ASCE 7-22 throughout. If you are pulling a Broward permit in 2026, the 8th Edition is what plan reviewers expect to see referenced on the wind load report. If a Broward project was designed under the 7th Edition and you are now applying for a permit, FI-24 outlines when redesign to the 8th Edition is required versus when the prior edition submittal can stand.

Broward in the Florida HVHZ Landscape

Broward is the second of Florida's two HVHZ counties, sitting directly north of Miami-Dade and south of Palm Beach. Together, the three Atlantic coast counties define the densest hurricane-design corridor in the United States — but each has its own design wind speed and its own administrative regime. The grid below shows how Broward fits between its neighbors:

Miami-Dade County HVHZ

175 mph

Directly south of Broward. Same HVHZ rules but 5 mph higher design floor. Plan review centralized under Miami-Dade RER; NOA originates here and is accepted statewide for HVHZ work.

Broward County HVHZ

170 mph

This page. Entire county at 170 mph. Plan review distributed across 31 municipalities, coordinated by Broward County Building Code Services Division. NOA + TAS 201/202/203 required for all openings.

Palm Beach County

165–170 mph

Directly north of Broward. NOT HVHZ — uses Florida statewide Product Approval (FL#) rather than HVHZ-specific NOA. Coastal ZIPs at top of range, inland slightly lower.

Collier County

170 mph

Gulf coast (Naples, Marco Island). Same 170 mph as Broward but NOT HVHZ — Florida statewide approval applies. Often used as the SW Florida comparable when sourcing products.

Practically speaking, the cleanest way to source impact-rated products for a Broward project is to specify HVHZ-rated assemblies — those are accepted everywhere in Florida, including Broward and Miami-Dade. The reverse is not true: a non-HVHZ Florida Product Approval will not cover a Broward opening even at the same wind speed, because the missing piece is the test protocol, not the pressure rating.

Broward County Building Code Services Division

The Building Code Services Division at the county level publishes Broward-specific amendments, FI bulletins, and inspector field directives that supplement the statewide Florida Building Code. Most actual plan review and permitting in Broward happens at the municipal level — each of the 31 incorporated cities operates its own building department under delegated authority — but the county is the umbrella authority and the appeal venue. Always confirm submittal forms with the specific municipality where the project sits; Fort Lauderdale's package differs from Hollywood's differs from unincorporated Broward.

Broward Wind Load FAQ

Is Broward County in the HVHZ?
Yes. Broward is one of only two High Velocity Hurricane Zone counties in Florida — Miami-Dade is the other. The entire county sits inside the HVHZ boundary, from the Atlantic coast at Hollywood and Fort Lauderdale all the way west to the Everglades line. That means every Broward jurisdiction — whether you are permitting in Pompano Beach, Coral Springs, Plantation, Davie, or unincorporated Broward — operates under HVHZ rules. There is no inland exemption inside the county. The Broward County Building Code Services Division administers the HVHZ provisions of the Florida Building Code with Broward-specific amendments layered on top.
What is the difference between Broward and Miami-Dade wind load requirements?
Both counties are HVHZ and both require NOA / TAS-tested products, but three differences matter on a permit submittal. First, the design wind speed: Broward uses 170 mph for Risk Category II, while Miami-Dade uses 175 mph. Second, the amendments: each county publishes its own technical bulletins (Broward through the Building Code Services Division, Miami-Dade through RER), and the two sets are not identical word-for-word — fire-rating cross-references, inspection sequencing, and submittal forms differ. Third, administration: Broward delegates a great deal of plan review authority to its 31 municipalities, while Miami-Dade keeps unincorporated review centralized. The same Miami-Dade NOA is accepted in Broward, but the calculation pressure target you have to meet is lower in Broward by virtue of the 170 vs 175 mph delta.
Do I need NOA for windows in Fort Lauderdale?
Yes. Fort Lauderdale is inside the Broward HVHZ, so any window, sliding glass door, entry door, garage door, or shutter installed in the city requires a Notice of Acceptance that explicitly covers HVHZ use. The NOA is most commonly issued by Miami-Dade County's Product Control Section, but Broward accepts it for use anywhere in the county. On the wind load report, each opening must be matched to an FL# (Florida Product Approval number) whose underlying NOA shows TAS 201 (Large Missile Impact), TAS 202 (Uniform Static Air Pressure), and TAS 203 (Cyclic Wind Pressure) test results meeting or exceeding the design pressure the calculator returns for that opening's zone.
Why is Broward 170 mph when Miami-Dade is 175 mph?
The 5 mph difference comes from how each county calibrated its post-Hurricane Andrew adoption. Miami-Dade, which took the most concentrated damage from Andrew in 1992, set a 175 mph design floor based on its own forensic engineering review. Broward sits one county north, took heavy but not catastrophic Andrew damage, and adopted 170 mph after a parallel but slightly less aggressive analysis. ASCE 7-22 baseline maps actually show similar values for both counties, but each county's adopted floor sits a few mph above the baseline to add margin. The numbers are not arbitrary — they correspond to a specific return-period and importance-factor calibration each county engineering office defended in adoption hearings.
What is the Broward FI-24 bulletin?
Florida Building Information (FI) bulletins are technical clarifications issued by Florida code authorities to interpret specific provisions of the Florida Building Code. The FI-24 bulletin addresses how the 8th Edition (2023) is to be applied in Broward, including transition rules from the 7th Edition, clarifications on HVHZ amendments, and guidance on product approval matching. If you are pulling a permit in Broward in 2026 and your project was designed under earlier guidance, FI-24 is the current Broward-authority document plan reviewers will reference. Cite it on submittals where your design predates the 8th Edition effective date but the permit application is being filed after it.
Do I need a PE stamp for Broward permits?
It depends on scope and stories. Broward generally requires a Florida-licensed PE signature and seal on wind load calculations for new construction, additions, and most commercial work. Residential window and door replacement permits can often be filed with a manufacturer's installation instructions plus a wind load report referencing FL# approvals, without a separate PE seal, provided the design pressures are met. WindLoadCalc's in-house PE sign-and-seal service is available for Florida projects up to three stories — that covers most single-family, townhouse, and small commercial work in Broward, but it does not cover the high-rise condos and hotels along the Fort Lauderdale beach corridor. For mid- and high-rise Broward projects, you will need a structural engineer of record.
Are storm panels acceptable in Broward?
Yes, storm panels are accepted in Broward as long as they carry an NOA that covers HVHZ use and the FL# is matched to each opening on the wind load report. The most common HVHZ-rated storm panel systems are aluminum corrugated panels, clear polycarbonate panels, and accordion shutters. Each system has tested design pressures that must equal or exceed the C&C pressure the calculator returns for the opening's zone (Zone 4 wall field, Zone 5 wall corner — corners typically govern). Broward plan reviewers will reject a submittal where the listed FL# storm panel rating is below the calculated design pressure, so the opening-by-opening match must be explicit on the report.
What is the cost difference between NOA-rated impact glass and storm panels in Broward?
For a typical Broward single-family home, HVHZ-rated impact glass runs roughly $60 to $110 per square foot installed depending on product line, frame material, and DP rating. NOA-rated storm panels for the same openings come in much lower at around $15 to $30 per square foot, but they require manual deployment before each storm event. Accordion shutters fall in between at $25 to $45 per square foot and deploy quickly without removing panels from storage. The break-even depends on how often deployment is needed, insurance discount eligibility (impact glass usually wins the larger discount), and resale considerations. The wind load report itself is identical for either route — design pressures are governed by the building, not the protection method.
