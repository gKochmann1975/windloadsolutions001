Collier County sits at the southwest tip of mainland Florida and routinely takes the brunt of Gulf hurricanes that intensify in the warm, shallow water immediately offshore. Hurricane Donna in 1960, Wilma in 2005, Irma in 2017, and Ian in 2022 all delivered sustained Category 3 or stronger winds somewhere inside the county line. That hurricane record is the reason Collier carries a 170 mph design wind speed — about 10 to 20 mph above what an unmodified ASCE 7-22 map lookup would return for southwest Florida — and the reason every Collier County permit submittal needs a wind load calculation that knows the local override exists.

Collier County is NOT in the HVHZ

One of the most common mistakes on Collier permit packages is treating the project as if it were in the High Velocity Hurricane Zone. HVHZ only covers Miami-Dade and Broward — the two counties on Florida's southeast coast. Collier follows the statewide Florida Building Code with Florida Product Approval (FL#) for windows, doors, shutters, and similar regulated assemblies. NOA approvals from Miami-Dade are accepted in Collier but are not required, and there is no TAS 201/202/203 testing mandate. The wind speed is still high — 170 mph — but the legal product approval pathway is the statewide FL# system.

Collier Local Code Context

Collier County permits run through the Collier County Growth Management Department in Naples, with separate plan review for the City of Naples and the City of Marco Island within their respective municipal boundaries. The applicable wind load standard is the Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023), which references ASCE 7-22, supplemented by the Collier County Land Development Code for zoning, setbacks, lot coverage, and accessory structure requirements that don't directly affect wind loads but do gate the permit.

A few Collier-specific items worth knowing before you submit:

  • Windborne Debris Region. All of Collier sits inside the FBC Windborne Debris Region, so every glazed opening in new construction and substantial renovations must be protected — either with impact-rated windows and doors or with code-compliant hurricane shutters (accordion, roll-down, panel, or in limited cases plywood).
  • Florida Product Approval (FL#), not NOA. Each window, door, shutter, and roofing assembly must carry a current Florida Product Approval number. The wind load report identifies the design pressure for each opening so the contractor can match a compliant FL# from the state database. Miami-Dade NOAs are accepted but not required.
  • Coastal Construction Control Line (CCCL). Beachfront parcels on Naples beaches and Marco Island may sit seaward of the Florida CCCL, which adds a separate Florida Department of Environmental Protection (FDEP) permit on top of the county building permit. CCCL projects typically run Exposure D and may need Risk Category bumps for primary frame elements.
  • Lanai and screen enclosure requirements. Collier residential lanais must be designed for the full 170 mph wind speed. Screen mesh selection (typical 18x14 fiberglass vs. 17x20 or hurricane mesh) drives the panel pressure rating; frame and footing design is governed by the C&C pressures on the structural members.
  • Edge strip minimum. Florida requires a 4 ft minimum edge strip dimension for C&C zone calculations (FBC R301.2(7)), which is larger than the 3 ft minimum used in most other states. WindLoadCalc applies the 4 ft minimum automatically for Florida projects, including Collier.

FBC 8th Edition (2023) and ASCE 7-22

The Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) took effect statewide on December 31, 2024. For Collier County, the most consequential change from FBC 7th Edition is the underlying ASCE reference — FBC 8th Edition adopts ASCE 7-22 instead of ASCE 7-16, which means new wind speed maps, a new "Partially Open" enclosure classification, and a reorganized Chapter 30 for Components and Cladding procedures.

What changed that specifically matters for Collier projects:

  • Partially Open enclosure type. ASCE 7-22 recognizes four enclosure classifications (Enclosed, Partially Open, Partially Enclosed, Open) where ASCE 7-16 had three. Many Naples lanais, pool cages, and carports fit the new Partially Open category, which uses a different internal pressure coefficient (GCpi = ±0.18) than the Open or Partially Enclosed classifications. Getting this right meaningfully changes the C&C pressures on the frame.
  • Updated Components and Cladding procedures. Chapter 30 of ASCE 7-22 reorganized the C&C flow for six different roof geometries (gable, hip, monoslope, multispan, sawtooth, stepped). The calculator handles all six and applies the correct zone pressures for the roof shape on your Collier project.
  • Roof zone geometry. ASCE 7-22 expanded roof zones for hip and gable roofs, which produces a different distribution of pressures across the roof field, edge, and corner than 7-16 did. Naples plan reviewers working under FBC 8th Edition expect the 7-22 zones on the submitted report.
  • Continuous load path. FBC 8th Edition continues to require a documented continuous load path from roof to foundation, which is most visible in the connector selection (Simpson Strong-Tie or equivalent) on residential framing details.

Why Collier's 170 mph Override Exists

If you look at the baseline ASCE 7-22 wind speed map for southwest Florida and trace inland from the Gulf coast, the contours generally fall in the 150 to 160 mph range for Risk Category II. The Collier County override pushes that up to 170 mph. The reason is not theoretical conservatism — it is observed building performance during four specific storms.

  • Hurricane Donna (1960) made landfall in Naples as a strong Category 4 and produced widespread structural damage in the era's lightly framed residential building stock.
  • Hurricane Wilma (2005) crossed the state from west to east and produced sustained Cat 3 winds in central Collier, exposing weaknesses in the soffit, roof covering, and opening protection details that the post-2005 code revisions addressed.
  • Hurricane Irma (2017) made landfall on Marco Island as a Category 4 with sustained winds near 130 mph and gusts above 140 mph, producing significant lanai, screen enclosure, and roof covering damage even on relatively new construction.
  • Hurricane Ian (2022) made landfall in adjacent Lee County but delivered Cat 3 winds and a 5-7 ft storm surge to coastal Collier, particularly Naples, Marco Island, and Goodland. Post-storm assessments drove tightening of the Collier code application.

The 170 mph design wind speed is calibrated to those four events plus the broader regional climatology. Because the override is set at the county level, it applies uniformly across every Collier ZIP whether your project is on the beach or 25 miles inland in Golden Gate Estates. The WindLoadCalc velocity finder applies the override automatically — you don't have to know it exists.

A product approved for use in Collier (FL# only) is not automatically approved for use in Broward (needs NOA in addition). A product approved for Broward (NOA + matching FL#) is fine for use in Collier. Always check both the FL# and the local pathway before specifying.

Collier County Wind Load FAQ

Is Collier County in the HVHZ?
No. Collier County is not part of the High Velocity Hurricane Zone. HVHZ is a specific designation in the Florida Building Code that covers only Miami-Dade and Broward counties. That distinction matters because HVHZ triggers a separate set of rules — Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) product approvals and TAS 201/202/203 testing — that do not legally apply in Collier. Collier follows the Florida statewide FBC 8th Edition with the Florida Product Approval system (FL#) for windows, doors, shutters, and other regulated assemblies. Collier projects still use a high 170 mph design wind speed, but the product approval pathway is statewide, not HVHZ-specific.
What is the design wind speed in Naples, Florida?
Naples uses a 170 mph design wind speed for Risk Category II buildings under ASCE 7-22 and Florida Building Code 8th Edition. This value applies across all Naples ZIP codes — 34102 (Old Naples), 34103 (Park Shore / Moorings), 34108 (North Naples / Vanderbilt), 34109, 34110, 34112, 34114, 34117 (Naples agricultural / east), 34119 (Naples east) — because the Collier County design wind speed is set at the county level. Risk Category III (assembly, schools) and Risk Category IV (hospitals, EOCs) buildings use a higher speed derived from the same county basis. Always confirm the exact value for your project ZIP in the calculator.
Why is Collier County 170 mph when SW Florida is generally lower?
The baseline ASCE 7-22 map for southwest Florida (Lee, Charlotte, Sarasota) typically returns design wind speeds in the 150 to 160 mph range for Risk Category II. Collier sits at the southern tip of that region, directly exposed to Gulf hurricane tracks that have repeatedly intensified before landfall — Donna (1960), Wilma (2005), Irma (2017), and Ian (2022) all produced sustained Cat 3+ winds in Collier. The county adopted a 170 mph local design wind speed as a jurisdiction override above the ASCE baseline, calibrated to actual observed building performance during those storms. The Collier override applies to every ZIP in the county and is built into the WindLoadCalc velocity finder.
Do I need a PE stamp for a Naples residential permit?
For most Collier County residential permits (window and door replacement, hurricane shutters, screen enclosures, lanais, pool cages, accessory structures) the wind load report by itself is not required to carry a PE seal — it just has to be calculated to FBC 8th Edition standards and submitted as part of the product approval package. Larger scopes (new single-family construction, structural alterations, raised lanais with new columns and beams) typically do require a Florida PE seal. We offer PE sign-and-seal for Florida residential and small commercial projects up to 3 stories, which covers most Collier County work.
What is the lanai wind load requirement in Naples?
A screened lanai or pool enclosure in Naples is designed for the 170 mph Collier County wind speed, applied as Components and Cladding (C&C) pressures to the screen mesh, framing members, and attachment points. Because the structure is typically open or partially open, ASCE 7-22 internal pressure coefficient (GCpi) selection matters — a partially open lanai uses different GCpi than an enclosed structure. WindLoadCalc handles all four ASCE 7-22 enclosure classifications (Enclosed, Partially Open, Partially Enclosed, Open) and produces the correct pressures for both the screen panels and the structural frame. Raised lanais (those built on an elevated platform with new columns and a roof) get full structural wind loads on every frame component.
Are storm shutters required in Collier County?
Collier County is in the FBC Windborne Debris Region, so all glazed openings in new construction and substantial renovations must be protected. Protection can be achieved with impact-rated windows and doors (large missile impact, ASTM E 1996 / E 1886), accordion or roll-down hurricane shutters, panel shutters, or in some limited cases plywood per the FBC alternate provisions. Unlike Miami-Dade or Broward, Collier does not require Miami-Dade NOA products — FL Product Approval is the statewide pathway. The wind load report you submit needs to list the design pressure for each opening so the contractor and plan reviewer can match it to a compliant FL#-approved product.
What is the difference between Naples and Marco Island wind requirements?
Both Naples and Marco Island sit inside Collier County and share the same 170 mph design wind speed at the Risk Category II level. The practical difference is exposure category. Marco Island (34145) sits directly on the Gulf with very little upwind obstruction, which often triggers Exposure D for sites within one mile of the open coastline. Many Naples ZIPs (34102, 34103) also qualify for Exposure D along the immediate beachfront but transition to Exposure C a short distance inland. Exposure D produces noticeably higher design pressures than C at the same wind speed, so the exposure call can change the calculated pressures by 15 to 25 percent. The calculator prompts you for exposure category and applies it correctly.
Does Collier require Miami-Dade NOA products?
No. Collier County is not HVHZ and does not legally require Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) approvals on windows, doors, shutters, or roofing assemblies. Collier accepts the Florida statewide Product Approval system, where each compliant product is identified by an FL# in the Florida Building Commission database. Many products carry both an FL# and a Miami-Dade NOA — those are fine to use in Collier — but the NOA is not a substitute or a prerequisite. Contractors and plan reviewers should pull the FL# for each opening from the database and confirm the design pressure rating meets or exceeds the calculated C&C pressure for that opening's zone.
