High Velocity Hurricane Zone — Miami-Dade is the Origin

The High Velocity Hurricane Zone is not a feature added to the Florida Building Code; it is the foundation the rest of the code was built on. Miami-Dade was the first jurisdiction in the United States to mandate impact testing, cyclic pressure testing, and county-issued Notices of Acceptance for building envelope products. When Hurricane Andrew made landfall on August 24, 1992 as a Category 5 storm with estimated peak gusts above 165 mph in south Miami-Dade, the existing South Florida Building Code (then administered separately from the rest of the state) failed catastrophically. Roofs lifted; gable ends pushed in; openings breached and internal pressure built up faster than structures could vent. The forensic investigation that followed concluded that the failure was not random — it was systemic, and the code itself needed restructuring.

The Miami-Dade response was the original HVHZ amendment package, which later became the template for the statewide Florida Building Code. Inside Miami-Dade (and Broward, the second HVHZ county), three jurisdiction-specific requirements apply:

  • Notice of Acceptance (NOA). Every product in the building envelope — windows, doors, shutters, roofing assemblies, soffit, vents, garage doors — must carry a current Miami-Dade County NOA. The NOA number appears on the product label and is searchable in the Miami-Dade product approval database. Statewide Florida Product Approval (FL#) by itself is not sufficient for an HVHZ submittal.
  • TAS 201 — Large Missile Impact. A 9 lb 2x4 lumber projectile fired at the product at 50 ft/s (or 80 ft/s for higher-rated assemblies). Most openings up to 30 ft above grade must pass Large Missile testing or be permanently protected by an NOA-approved shutter. This is the single most expensive HVHZ requirement; impact-rated glazing typically costs 2 to 3 times the price of non-impact glazing.
  • TAS 202 / TAS 203 — Uniform Static + Cyclic Pressure. TAS 202 verifies the assembly can resist sustained design pressure without permanent deformation or air/water infiltration beyond limits. TAS 203 then subjects it to thousands of cycles of pressure reversal, simulating the gust-to-gust loading of a multi-hour hurricane. Many products that pass static pressure fail cyclic — TAS 203 is the test that exposes weak fasteners, sealants, and frame corners.

Permit authority for all of this sits with the Miami-Dade County Building Code Compliance Office for unincorporated areas, and with the local municipal building department for the incorporated cities (Miami, Miami Beach, Coral Gables, Hialeah, Aventura, Doral, Homestead, and the smaller municipalities). All of them enforce the same Miami-Dade HVHZ amendments to the Florida Building Code, so the product approval pathway is the same regardless of which jurisdiction issues the permit.

FBC 8th Edition (2023) + Miami-Dade HVHZ Amendments

The Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) took effect December 31, 2024 and references ASCE 7-22 as the wind load standard. Miami-Dade's HVHZ amendments sit on top of the statewide code — they do not replace it. The base ASCE 7-22 procedures still govern the calculation method (Chapter 26 general provisions, Chapter 27 MWFRS Directional, Chapter 28 MWFRS Envelope, Chapter 30 Components and Cladding), but the 175 mph local design wind speed and the NOA / TAS product approval requirements supersede the rest-of-state defaults.

The procedural changes between ASCE 7-16 and ASCE 7-22 that matter most for Miami-Dade submittals:

  • Partially Open enclosure classification. ASCE 7-22 recognizes four enclosure types — Enclosed, Partially Open, Partially Enclosed, and Open — where ASCE 7-16 had three. Many Miami-Dade buildings with screened lanais, open garage bays, or hurricane shutters in the partially-deployed position now fall into the Partially Open category, which changes the GCpi internal pressure coefficient and shifts the calculated design pressures.
  • Chapter 30 C&C reorganization. The new chapter restructures the C&C calculation flow for the six common roof shapes (gable, hip, monoslope, sawtooth, multispan, dome). For Miami-Dade flat-roof low-rise commercial — a very common roof type — the zone definitions and effective wind area curves have been refined.
  • Edge strip "a" dimension. Florida (per FBC R301.2(7)) requires a 4 ft minimum edge strip dimension, where ASCE 7-22 elsewhere allows 3 ft. The calculator applies the 4 ft minimum automatically for any Florida ZIP, including all of Miami-Dade.
  • Wind speed map refinements. The underlying ASCE 7-22 map shifted some coastal Florida contour lines based on updated hurricane climatology. For Miami-Dade specifically, the local 175 mph override dominates either way, but the underlying map value matters when deriving Risk Category III and IV design speeds.

Why Miami-Dade is 175 mph, Not the ASCE Baseline

The baseline ASCE 7-22 wind speed map for southeast Florida produces values in the 165 to 170 mph range for Risk Category II at most south Florida ZIP codes. Miami-Dade has chosen to design at 175 mph, a meaningful step up from the map. Two things drive this local override.

First, exposure density. Miami-Dade has the highest density of high-rise residential construction in the southeast United States. A failure mode that produces an acceptable casualty rate in a rural area becomes unacceptable when the failure happens in a high-rise corridor with tens of thousands of people in a half-mile radius. The post-Andrew, post-Wilma, and post-Irma forensic engineering investigations all reached the same conclusion: the building stock in Miami-Dade was overconcentrated relative to the underlying design assumptions, and the design wind speed needed to be set conservatively above the underlying map.

Second, the cost-benefit of incremental design pressure in this specific geography is overwhelmingly favorable. Going from 170 to 175 mph in design wind speed adds a single-digit percentage to construction cost on most building types, but it materially reduces the expected damage from a 100-year storm event. For a county with the property exposure of Miami-Dade, that trade is obvious — and the insurance market reinforces it.

Miami-Dade Wind Load FAQ

What is a Miami-Dade NOA and when do I need one?
A Notice of Acceptance (NOA) is a product approval document issued by the Miami-Dade County Building Code Compliance Office. It certifies that a specific window, door, shutter, roof tile, roofing underlayment, or other building envelope assembly has passed the Miami-Dade HVHZ test protocols and is approved for use inside the High Velocity Hurricane Zone. If your project is anywhere in Miami-Dade County, every component installed in the building envelope must be covered by a current NOA, identified by an NOA number printed on the product label and listed in the Miami-Dade product approval database. The plan reviewer will check each opening on your wind load report against an NOA before issuing a permit.
Do I need TAS 201 impact testing for every product in Miami-Dade?
Yes, for any product in the building envelope that is not behind permanent impact protection. TAS 201 is the Large Missile Impact test in which a 2x4 lumber projectile is fired at the product at 50 ft/s (or 80 ft/s for higher-rated assemblies). It is one of three mandatory HVHZ tests: TAS 201 (Large Missile Impact), TAS 202 (Uniform Static Air Pressure), and TAS 203 (Cyclic Wind Pressure). The only common exemptions are openings above 30 feet from grade that meet the small-missile criteria, and openings fully protected by an NOA-approved hurricane shutter. The calculator flags every opening that requires Large Missile Impact testing in the output report.
What is the difference between Miami-Dade requirements and broader Florida wind requirements?
Three things separate Miami-Dade from the rest of Florida. First, the design wind speed: Miami-Dade uses 175 mph for Risk Category II, the highest urban override in the state. Second, the product approval pathway: Miami-Dade requires a county-issued NOA, where most of Florida accepts the statewide Florida Product Approval (FL#) system. Third, the test protocols: Miami-Dade enforces TAS 201/202/203, which are stricter than the comparable AAMA and ASTM standards used elsewhere in Florida. A product approved for use in Naples or Sarasota is not automatically approved for use in Miami-Dade. Many manufacturers carry both ratings, but you must verify the NOA explicitly covers HVHZ installation.
Why is Miami-Dade 175 mph when the broader Florida baseline is around 170 mph?
Miami-Dade is denser and more exposed than any other urban county in Florida, and the failure mode of densely-packed structures in a major hurricane is fundamentally different from rural or low-density failure modes. After Hurricane Andrew (1992) leveled large sections of Homestead and south Miami-Dade, the county's post-storm forensic engineering investigations concluded that the design wind speed in use at the time was insufficient for the local exposure. The 175 mph value was set as a local override above whatever the underlying ASCE map produces, and it is now embedded in Miami-Dade HVHZ amendments to the Florida Building Code. The WindLoadCalc velocity finder applies this override automatically for every Miami-Dade ZIP code.
Are there exemptions for low-rise residential or small remodels in Miami-Dade?
No real exemption from the 175 mph design wind speed or the HVHZ product approval requirement, regardless of building size. A 1,200 sq ft single-family home remodel in Kendall is governed by the same wind speed and NOA requirements as a 40-story Brickell condo. There are some narrow scope exemptions — like-for-like cosmetic replacement of certain non-envelope components — but any window, door, or shutter replacement triggers HVHZ product approval review. The most common surprise for out-of-state contractors is that an impact window approved for use in Texas or the Carolinas is not automatically permittable in Miami-Dade; the product needs a Miami-Dade NOA, not just a generic ASTM E1996 rating.
Can I still use ASCE 7-16 for a Miami-Dade project instead of ASCE 7-22?
Only if the permit application was submitted under the previous code cycle. The Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) took effect December 31, 2024 and references ASCE 7-22. For permits submitted on or after that date, Miami-Dade plan reviewers will expect calculations under ASCE 7-22. The practical differences for Miami-Dade between ASCE 7-16 and 7-22 are relatively small (the 175 mph local override dominates the calculation either way), but the procedural changes — the new Partially Open enclosure classification, the reorganized Chapter 30 C&C procedures, and the 4 ft minimum edge strip 'a' dimension under FBC R301.2(7) — all need to be reflected in the submittal. WindLoadCalc runs under ASCE 7-22 by default.
Do I need a PE stamp on a Miami-Dade wind load report?
Miami-Dade plan review routinely requires a Florida-licensed PE seal on wind load calculations for any project beyond a straightforward like-for-like window or door replacement. For a residential remodel up to 3 stories, our in-house PE can seal the WindLoadCalc report directly. For Miami-Dade high-rise work — condos, mid-rise mixed-use, hotels — most projects are 4 or more stories, which falls outside the scope of our PE's licensure (our service is capped at 3 stories). In that case the calculator output is still useful as a working document, but the structural engineer of record on the project will need to seal the wind load analysis themselves. We can deliver the raw inputs, intermediate calculations, and pressure tables in a format their PE can review and adopt.
What's the difference between Miami-Dade Large Missile Level D and Level E?
The Miami-Dade missile level system rates impact resistance by the size and velocity of the test projectile. Level D is the standard Large Missile rating: a 9 lb 2x4 lumber projectile fired at 50 ft/s, used for most openings up to 30 ft above grade. Level E is the higher rating: the same 9 lb projectile fired at 80 ft/s, used for essential facilities, certain Risk Category III/IV projects, and any opening where the design analysis calls for it. Some glazing systems also carry separate Small Missile ratings (Levels A, B, C) for openings above 30 ft. The calculator includes Risk Category in its output so you can match the right impact level when selecting NOA-approved products.
