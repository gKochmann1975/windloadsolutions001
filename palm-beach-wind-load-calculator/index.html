The single most-misunderstood fact about Palm Beach permitting

Palm Beach County is not HVHZ. The HVHZ designation under the Florida Building Code applies only to Miami-Dade and Broward counties and stops at the Broward-Palm Beach county line in Boca Raton. This is purely a jurisdictional boundary — there is no geographic or climatic break — which is exactly why so many contractors get it wrong on their first Palm Beach permit. Florida statewide Product Approval (FL#) is the controlling system here, not Miami-Dade NOA.

Why Palm Beach Is Not HVHZ (And Why It Matters)

The Florida Building Code defines the High Velocity Hurricane Zone in Section 202 as Miami-Dade and Broward counties — full stop. Palm Beach County sits immediately north of Broward and shares the same Atlantic exposure, the same hurricane climatology, and the same construction practices for most projects. But the HVHZ designation is a regulatory choice that was made decades ago based on the population density and damage history of Miami-Dade and Broward specifically. It was never extended north into Palm Beach.

The practical consequences for a Palm Beach permit submittal are significant:

  • Product approval pathway. Palm Beach permits reference Florida statewide Product Approval — the FL# system maintained by the Florida Building Commission. Miami-Dade NOA is not legally required. If a manufacturer's data sheet shows only an FL#, it is acceptable in Palm Beach; if it shows only a Miami-Dade NOA without an FL#, it is technically not.
  • Testing requirements. TAS 201 (Large Missile Impact), TAS 202 (Uniform Static Air Pressure), and TAS 203 (Cyclic Wind Pressure) — the HVHZ test suite — are not mandatory in Palm Beach. Products do still need to meet ASTM E1996/E1886 impact criteria for the Wind-Borne Debris Region, but the specific TAS protocols are an HVHZ requirement.
  • Cost. HVHZ-rated products run roughly 2 to 3 times the cost of comparable rest-of-state impact products. Specifying HVHZ-only in Palm Beach over-buys by a meaningful margin on most projects, particularly large fenestration packages.
  • Plan reviewer expectations. Palm Beach County Planning, Zoning & Building Department and the municipal building departments (West Palm Beach, Boca Raton, Delray Beach, Jupiter, Boynton Beach, Wellington, Palm Beach Gardens) all review against the FBC 8th Edition statewide product approval pathway. A submittal that only references Miami-Dade NOAs is not wrong — it's just over-specified and may invite questions.

None of this means HVHZ-rated products are prohibited in Palm Beach. Plenty of manufacturers carry dual approval (an FL# alongside a Miami-Dade NOA on the same product line), and many Palm Beach homeowners specifically request HVHZ-rated assemblies for resale value, insurance discounts, or peace of mind. The point is simply that the calculator outputs FL# references because FL# is the controlling number for Palm Beach plan review.

Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) in Palm Beach

The Florida Building Code 8th Edition (2023) took effect statewide on December 31, 2024. Palm Beach County and all 38 municipalities within it adopted the 8th Edition on that date with no county-specific amendments to the wind load chapters. The structural and wind provisions reference ASCE 7-22 as the wind load standard. The relevant changes from the previous edition for Palm Beach work:

  • Updated ASCE 7-22 wind speed maps. The contour lines crossing Palm Beach shifted slightly compared to ASCE 7-16, generally a small uptick in design speed for coastal ZIPs reflecting updated hurricane climatology.
  • Four enclosure classifications. ASCE 7-22 recognizes Enclosed, Partially Open, Partially Enclosed, and Open. The "Partially Open" category is new versus 7-16's three categories and is directly relevant to Palm Beach lanais, screen enclosures, and the open-air pool cages that are standard on Boca Raton and Delray Beach single-family homes.
  • Chapter 30 C&C reorganization. The Components and Cladding calculation flow is restructured by roof shape; the calculator handles all six covered roof geometries.
  • Edge strip "a" minimum. Florida (per FBC R301.2(7)) holds the edge strip dimension to a 4 ft minimum even when the ASCE 7-22 formulation would allow 3 ft. This bumps C&C corner zone pressures on smaller residential structures and the calculator applies the 4 ft floor automatically for Florida ZIPs.

WindLoadCalc has been updated to FBC 8th Edition / ASCE 7-22 throughout. Palm Beach permits issued under FBC 7th Edition before the December 31, 2024 effective date can still be carried to completion under that earlier code — contact support if you need a retrofit calculation referencing the prior edition.

The Palm Beach Coastal-to-Inland Gradient

Most Florida county wind speed conversations end with a single number: Miami-Dade is 175, Broward is 170, Collier is 170. Palm Beach genuinely cannot be summarized that way. The county is about 30 miles east-to-west and roughly 60 miles north-to-south, and the ASCE 7-22 wind speed contours cross it diagonally. A project in oceanfront Highland Beach designs to roughly 170 mph; a project ten miles west in agricultural Loxahatchee Groves designs closer to 160 mph. Treating those two projects the same either over-designs the western one (and quotes the homeowner too high) or under-designs the eastern one (and risks a permit rejection).

WindLoadCalc reads the per-ZIP ASCE 7-22 value through its velocity finder, which applies any local override layered on top of the raw map. For Palm Beach there is no county-wide override the way Miami-Dade and Broward have one — the calculator returns the ASCE map value for that exact ZIP. Why the gradient matters for C&C pressures

Design pressure scales with the square of wind speed. The difference between 165 mph and 170 mph is only about a 6 percent jump in velocity but roughly a 12 percent jump in Components and Cladding pressure. On a large fenestration package — say, a 40-window beachfront home in Manalapan vs. an equivalent inland home in Wellington — that 12 percent difference moves the project across product approval tiers and can change which window lines qualify. Getting the per-ZIP value right is not a rounding-error concern in Palm Beach.

Palm Beach Wind Load FAQ

Is Palm Beach County in the HVHZ?
No. The High Velocity Hurricane Zone under the Florida Building Code covers only Miami-Dade and Broward counties. Palm Beach County sits immediately north of Broward and shares the same Atlantic exposure, but it is not designated HVHZ. This trips up a lot of contractors and homeowners because the HVHZ line is geographic and stops at the Broward-Palm Beach county border, not at any natural feature. The practical consequence: Florida statewide Product Approval (FL#) applies in Palm Beach, but the additional Miami-Dade NOA, TAS 201/202/203 testing, and HVHZ-specific product requirements are not legally required. Many products carry both ratings, so HVHZ-approved windows can still be used; they are just not mandatory.
What is the wind speed in Boca Raton vs West Palm Beach?
Both Boca Raton and West Palm Beach use Risk Category II design wind speeds in the 165 to 170 mph range under ASCE 7-22, but the exact value depends on how close the specific ZIP is to the Atlantic. Coastal ZIPs in Boca Raton (33432, 33483, the barrier island portion of 33480 in nearby Palm Beach) and oceanfront West Palm Beach typically pull the higher 170 mph value. Inland ZIPs like 33445 (western Delray), 33484, and the western edges of West Palm Beach (33409, 33411) sit closer to 165 mph. Always run the calculator on the actual project ZIP — the variation matters for fenestration design pressures.
Why does Palm Beach have a 165-170 mph range when Broward is a flat 170 mph?
Broward County adopted a single uniform 170 mph design wind speed as a county-wide HVHZ override. Palm Beach County did not adopt a flat county-wide override and instead follows the ASCE 7-22 wind speed map, which shows a measurable coastal-to-inland gradient across the county's roughly 30-mile east-west span. The wind contour lines on the ASCE map cross Palm Beach diagonally, so the southeast corner near the ocean reads about 170 mph while the agricultural western interior near Lake Okeechobee reads closer to 160-165 mph. The calculator handles this gradient at the ZIP level so coastal projects don't get under-designed and inland projects don't get over-designed.
Do I need impact windows in Boca Raton?
Yes — Boca Raton sits inside the Florida Wind-Borne Debris Region, which is defined by the Florida Building Code as any area where the design wind speed is 140 mph or higher. Boca Raton's 165-170 mph design speed puts it well above that threshold. Every opening in the building envelope must either be protected by an impact-rated product (impact glass, impact doors, impact garage doors) or by an approved shutter system (accordion, roll-down, panel, or fabric). Plywood is permitted only as temporary protection for one- and two-family dwellings under specific conditions. Florida Product Approval (FL#) numbers must be referenced on the permit drawings for whichever product you specify.
What is required for a beachfront permit in Palm Beach?
Beachfront and barrier-island projects in Palm Beach County trigger several layers above a standard inland permit. The wind load report should use Exposure D (within one mile of unobstructed open water) which produces noticeably higher C&C pressures than Exposure C. The county or municipality may require review by Florida DEP if the project is seaward of the Coastal Construction Control Line (CCCL). Florida Product Approval is required on every opening, and many beachfront municipalities (Town of Palm Beach, Manalapan, Highland Beach, Ocean Ridge) have local architectural review boards that add cosmetic requirements on top of the structural code. WindLoadCalc handles the Exposure D selection and pressure output; the CCCL review and architectural board steps are separate from the wind load calculation.
Are Miami-Dade NOA products required in Palm Beach?
No, Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) products are not legally required in Palm Beach County because Palm Beach is not in the HVHZ. Florida statewide Product Approval (the FL# system maintained by the Florida Building Commission) is the controlling product approval pathway in Palm Beach. That said, many manufacturers carry both an FL# and a Miami-Dade NOA on the same product line, so HVHZ-approved windows, doors, and shutters are commonly installed in Palm Beach by choice. Doing so is permitted, and some homeowners prefer it for resale value or insurance discounts. Just remember the calculator outputs FL# references because that is the controlling number for permit submittal in Palm Beach.
What is the lanai or pool cage requirement in Palm Beach?
Lanais, pool cages, and screen enclosures in Palm Beach County are typically designed as Open or Partially Open structures under ASCE 7-22, which changes the internal pressure coefficient (GCpi) compared to a fully enclosed structure. Aluminum-frame screen enclosures must be designed for the ZIP-level design wind speed (165-170 mph) but get a partial pressure reduction because air flows through the screen mesh. Roof panels and structural fasteners still see the full design pressure. Most Palm Beach municipalities require engineered drawings for any pool cage over a defined size threshold. The calculator handles all four ASCE 7-22 enclosure classifications (Enclosed, Partially Open, Partially Enclosed, Open) so you can model the lanai correctly.
Do I need a PE stamp for Palm Beach permits?
It depends on the project scope. Residential window and door replacement permits in Palm Beach County typically do not require a PE seal on the wind load report — the manufacturer's FL# product approval plus a code-compliant pressure calculation is sufficient. New construction, additions, structural alterations, and any commercial project of meaningful size will require a Florida-licensed PE or RA to seal the structural and wind load documents. WindLoadCalc offers PE sign-and-seal as an add-on service for Florida projects up to 3 stories. Many Palm Beach beachfront condos are 4 stories or taller and fall outside that scope — for those, an independent structural engineer of record is required.
